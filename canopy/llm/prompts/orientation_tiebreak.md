# DIRECTION OF THE MEASURE — A THIRD READING

Two readers have already answered this question about ONE measure in the attached paper and they
did not settle it: they disagreed, or one or both of them could not tell. You are the third
reader. Answer the same question, from the paper, for yourself.

What you are given that they were not: the raw group values this paper reports for the measure,
exactly as they were read, with no sign applied to them. They are here so that a claim about which
group came out higher can be checked against the numbers instead of remembered from the abstract.
They are NOT the answer — a group being higher says nothing about whether higher is more of the
construct, which is the whole of what is being asked.

Read the earlier ballots as evidence, not as votes to be counted. If one of them quotes a sentence
from the methods that settles the definition, go and read that sentence in the paper. If both of
them guessed, say so and answer `unknown`: an answer nothing in the paper supports is worse than
no answer, because the review will have no way of knowing it was a guess.

Answer three things.

1. `raw_value_semantics` — what a larger raw number is more of:
   * `higher_more_construct` — more of the quantity the outcome definition describes;
   * `higher_more_error` — more deviation, more mistake, more cost, more time to do a task;
   * `signed_direction` — the sign carries a direction, so the magnitude alone means nothing;
   * `unknown` — the paper does not let you tell.
2. `higher_is_better` — `higher`, `lower`, or `unknown`, read together with the outcome definition
   below: does a larger raw value on this measure mean MORE of what this review is measuring?
   Answer `unknown` rather than guessing; a wrong answer here inverts every result built on it.
3. `direction_stated_in_text` — which group the paper itself says came out higher **on this raw
   measure**: `a_greater`, `b_greater`, or `unknown` when the paper does not say. This is about the
   paper's own words, not about which group is better off, and not about the values printed below.

Put the verbatim words that establish your answer in `quotes` — the definition of the measure in
the Methods, the axis label or caption, the sentence that describes the result. Two or three short
quotes are better than one long one. In `reason`, say which of the earlier readers you agree with
and why, or say that neither of them is supported by anything the paper prints.
